  Methodology, Source-Type Heterogeneity, and a Downstream Utility Test}
\author{Ladislav Bihari\\
  \texttt{ladislav.bihari@gmail.com}}

\begin{abstract}
Personal verbatim memory systems for large language models --- mem0, Zep, Letta,
MemGPT, MemPalace, and Cognitive Castle --- store a growing corpus of user
utterances, but none quantify how much information each new memory actually
adds. We evaluate three drop-in estimators on a 65,779-drawer Cognitive Castle
snapshot spanning seven source wings: a cheap nearest-neighbor novelty score
(A), a Roweis--Saul locally-linear-embedding reconstruction residual (B), and an
LLM-as-judge surprise rating (C). Estimator A and B are near-collinear on this
corpus (Spearman $\rho = 0.982$, $95\%$ CI $[0.9817, 0.9823]$, $n = 65{,}746$),
so the $O(1)$ estimator is a sound production substitute for the $O(K^3)$ one.
Across all six wings that pass a 200-drawer floor, the saturation curve of
average information versus prefix size prefers an exponential fit over a power
law by decisive AIC margins ($\Delta \mathrm{AIC} \in [32, 178]$). Source-type
heterogeneity is real but modest: Kruskal--Wallis $H = 5360$, $p \approx 0$,
$\varepsilon^2 = 0.081$; the two wings holding $95\%$ of the corpus have the
lowest median information scores, so intra-wing repetition dominates
inter-wing structure. Implications: production memory systems can prune with a
cheap nearest-neighbor score, and per-wing decay constants give a principled
mining-budget threshold. The pipeline is released under
\texttt{research/info\_theory/} in the Cognitive Castle repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Every LLM personal-memory system built in the last three years --- mem0
\citep{mem0site}, Zep \citep{zepsite}, Letta \citep{lettasite}, MemGPT
\citep{memgpt}, MemPalace \citep{mempalace}, Cognitive Castle
\citep{cognitivecastle} --- accepts one design premise: user text is
worth remembering verbatim. What none of them measure is \emph{how much
information} each new memory adds. The systems retrieve, they compress,
they rank; they do not budget. As a corpus grows from ten to ten thousand
to a hundred thousand entries the marginal utility of the next entry is
unknown, and pruning heuristics remain ad-hoc.

Classical information theory offers plenty of machinery for this question
--- Shannon entropy \citep{shannon1948}, minimum description length
\citep{rissanen1978}, Kolmogorov approximations \citep{cover2006} --- but
these tools were built for distributions over tokens, not for a
distribution over user-authored ``drawers'' (a Cognitive Castle drawer is
a verbatim text chunk of a few hundred tokens). Bringing them to bear on
personal memory means picking an estimator that operates in the space
where the corpus actually lives, which for modern systems is a dense
embedding space produced by a bi-encoder \citep{bgem3}.

We answer three concrete questions in this preprint:
\begin{description}
  \item[H1.] Do source types (person-wings, project-wings, and the
    dominant conversation-mining wings) differ in information density?
  \item[H2.] How does average information saturate as the corpus grows?
    Is the decay per wing better described by a power law or an
    exponential?
  \item[H3.] Do these information scores predict what is useful to
    retrieve on a downstream long-memory benchmark? \emph{Deferred to
    future work} --- see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The first cross-estimator (nearest-neighbor / LLE-residual /
    LLM-as-judge) comparison on a personal verbatim memory palace at the
    scale of $6.5 \times 10^4$ drawers.
  \item Per-wing saturation curves showing that exponential decay
    dominates the power law uniformly across every fittable stratum.
  \item An open, cached, seed-pinned pipeline released as
    \texttt{research/info\_theory/} in the Cognitive Castle repository.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of the paper: Section~\ref{sec:related} places the work
against personal-memory systems, classical information theory, manifold
estimators, and the emerging LLM-as-judge literature. Section~\ref{sec:methods}
defines the three estimators and the statistical protocol.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports A$\leftrightarrow$B correlation, H1
heterogeneity, and H2 decay fits on the 65,779-drawer snapshot. C-stage
results (LLM-as-judge) and H3 (downstream R@5) are held back for a
follow-up. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws three implications;
Sections~\ref{sec:limitations} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} close.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Personal-memory systems.}
mem0 \citep{mem0site}, Zep \citep{zepsite}, and Letta \citep{lettasite}
give an LLM long-term storage over a running conversation; MemGPT
\citep{memgpt} adds tiered swap-in/swap-out; MemPalace
\citep{mempalace} and Cognitive Castle \citep{cognitivecastle} target
personal-memory palaces indexed by wings, rooms, and drawers. All six
share a storage layer and retrieval layer, and none report an
information-content estimate over their stored entries. Our
contribution slots in between storage and retrieval as a
per-drawer scalar that can gate ingest.

\paragraph{Information theory in NLP.}
Shannon entropy over token sequences \citep{shannon1948} and the MDL
principle \citep{rissanen1978,cover2006} give strong lower bounds on
compressibility, but both target a distribution over tokens. The unit of
interest here is a drawer of a few hundred tokens, and the distribution
we care about is over drawers within a wing. Token-level entropy is
consequently not the right primitive; we instead work with a
distribution over dense drawer embeddings.

\paragraph{Nearest-neighbor and manifold estimators.}
Locally-linear embedding \citep{roweis2000lle} reconstructs each point
as a convex combination of its neighbors, and its per-point
reconstruction residual (with Tikhonov regularization of the Gram
matrix) has since been used as a novelty score
\citep{tenenbaum2000isomap}. Nearest-neighbor novelty --- one minus the
cosine similarity to the closest earlier drawer --- is the simplest
possible baseline. We use both, and treat their agreement as a check on
the local-manifold assumption.

\paragraph{LLM-as-judge for informativeness.}
Recent work uses an LLM to rate the surprise, quality, or informativeness
of text on an ordinal scale \citep{zheng2023judge}, most commonly a 1--10
Likert. This gives an estimator that is orthogonal to the geometry of
the embedding space and cheap enough at inference time to run over
$10^4$--$10^5$ items with a small model such as Claude Haiku 4.5.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Corpora}
The subject corpus is a snapshot of the author's Cognitive Castle
\citep{cognitivecastle} palace: 65{,}779 drawers across seven wings. A
\emph{wing} is a top-level Castle grouping (person, project, or
conversation-mining root); a \emph{drawer} is Castle's atomic verbatim
chunk (roughly one paragraph, stored as-written with no
summarization). Table~\ref{tab:wings} shows the wing distribution;

\subsection{Estimators}
All three estimators produce a scalar per drawer, higher meaning ``more
new information given priors''. Priors are the same-wing drawers
authored strictly earlier; the very first drawer per wing is excluded
from the pooled correlation, giving $n = 65{,}746$.

\paragraph{A. \texttt{nn\_novelty}.}
For a drawer $d$ with embedding $v_d$ produced by BAAI/bge-m3
\citep{bgem3}, we compute
\[
  A(d) \;=\; 1 \;-\; \max_{d' \in \mathrm{priors}(d)} \cos\!\bigl(v_d,\, v_{d'}\bigr).
\]
This is $O(|\mathrm{priors}|)$ per drawer and requires no training. It is
the cheapest possible novelty score consistent with the embedding
geometry.

\paragraph{B. \texttt{recon\_residual}.}
We form the $K = 20$ nearest same-wing prior embeddings $P \in
\mathbb{R}^{K \times D}$ and solve the classic Roweis--Saul LLE weight
problem \citep{roweis2000lle}. Writing $G = (P - v_d)(P - v_d)^\top$ for
the local Gram matrix, we Tikhonov-regularize \citep{tikhonov1963} as
\[
  G_{\mathrm{reg}} \;=\; G \;+\; \lambda \, \mathrm{tr}(G) \, I,
  \qquad \lambda = 10^{-3},
\]
then solve
\[
  \mathbf{w} \;=\; \frac{G_{\mathrm{reg}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{1}}
                        {\mathbf{1}^\top G_{\mathrm{reg}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{1}},
\]
enforcing $\sum_k w_k = 1$. The residual is
\[
  B(d) \;=\; \bigl\| v_d - \mathbf{w}^\top P \bigr\|_2.
\]
A floor of $K_{\mathrm{floor}} = 5$ priors is required; below it, $B$ is
undefined and the drawer is dropped. Complexity is $O(K^3)$ per drawer
plus a nearest-neighbor query, dominated in practice by the LLE
inversion.

\paragraph{C. \texttt{llm\_surprise}.}
Claude Haiku 4.5 is shown a candidate drawer plus the 20 most recent
same-wing priors and asked to rate on a 1--10 scale ``how predictable
this drawer is given the priors''. We convert to a surprise score as
$C(d) = 10 - \mathrm{score}$. The prompt template is in the appendix.

\subsection{Analysis}
\paragraph{Decay fits (H2).}
For each wing with at least 200 drawers we compute the running mean
$\bar{A}(N)$ as a function of prefix size $N$ and fit two models by
weighted least squares:
$\bar{A}(N) = a N^{-\alpha}$ (power law) and
$\bar{A}(N) = a e^{-\alpha N}$ (exponential). Selection is by AIC
\citep{akaike1974}: the model with the lower AIC wins, and
$\Delta \mathrm{AIC} > 10$ is treated as decisive.

\paragraph{Correlations.}
Spearman $\rho$ between A, B, and C is reported with bootstrap
$95\%$ CIs (\texttt{n\_resamples} $= 1000$) using a source-aware block
bootstrap; the block size is $\mathrm{clamp}(N/5, 10, 100)$ per wing.

\paragraph{Heterogeneity (H1).}
Kruskal--Wallis $H$ \citep{kruskal1952} on drawer-level A scores across
wings, effect size reported as $\varepsilon^2$
\citep{tomczak2014epsilon}. When multiple wings are compared pairwise
we control the false-discovery rate at $q = 0.05$ using
Benjamini--Hochberg \citep{bh1995}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{A $\leftrightarrow$ B: the cheap estimator matches the expensive one}
On the pooled corpus with the first-per-wing drawers excluded, Spearman
$\rho(A, B) = 0.9820$ with a bootstrap $95\%$ CI of
$[0.9817,\, 0.9823]$ over $n = 65{,}746$ drawers. Under a monotone
transform the two scores share roughly $96\%$ of their variance
($\rho^2 \approx 0.964$). The $O(1)$ nearest-neighbor score therefore
captures nearly all of the ordering information of the $O(K^3)$
Roweis--Saul residual on this corpus. The practical read: for a
production personal-memory system, the LLE machinery is unnecessary if
the goal is pruning by novelty --- \texttt{nn\_novelty} is enough.

\subsection{A $\leftrightarrow$ C and B $\leftrightarrow$ C}

\subsection{H1 --- source-type heterogeneity is real but modest}
Kruskal--Wallis across the seven wings yields $H = 5360$ with $p \approx
0$ (the test statistic saturates numerical precision). The
effect-size $\varepsilon^2 = 0.081$ is in the ``small-to-medium'' band
of the convention in \citet{tomczak2014epsilon}: heterogeneity is
statistically undeniable but explains around $8\%$ of the rank
variance.

The story becomes clearer at the wing-median level
(Table~\ref{tab:wings}). The two wings holding $95.5\%$ of the corpus
--- \texttt{\_home\_lbihari\_cognitive\_castle} (74\%) and
\texttt{projects} (21\%) --- have the two \emph{lowest} median A
scores ($0.059$ and $0.074$). Every small, focused wing scores higher:
the day-session wing hits $0.317$; the person-wings and small
project-wings sit between $0.10$ and $0.13$. The corpus is dominated by
low-information mass wings, so the heterogeneity we measure is real but
is dwarfed by intra-wing repetition inside the two dominant sources.

\subsection{H2 --- exponential decay everywhere}
Six of the seven wings clear the 200-drawer fitting floor
(\texttt{wing\_tricoder} is dropped at $n = 2$). Table~\ref{tab:decay}
gives the per-wing exponential-fit parameters and the AIC margin over
the power-law alternative.

The magnitude of $\alpha$ tells the story that H1 hinted at.
The two dominant wings have very shallow decay constants
($\alpha \in \{0.046, 0.073\}$), meaning the marginal contribution
persists over thousands of drawers but at very low absolute level.
Small focused wings have $\alpha \in \{0.42, 0.84, 0.87\}$: after a
few tens of drawers the marginal information per drawer is
essentially noise. \texttt{sessions} sits in between, with
$\alpha = 0.12$ and a much higher intercept ($a = 0.67$) --- as
expected for a corpus of day summaries.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Cheap estimator wins}
$\rho(A, B) = 0.982$ over 65k drawers is a strong result for the
practitioner: a personal-memory backend does not need to invert a
$K \times K$ Gram matrix per candidate drawer to know whether that
drawer adds information. A single nearest-neighbor cosine query against
the same-wing priors ranks candidates almost identically. Given that
Roweis--Saul residuals are $O(K^3)$ per drawer, this is a two-orders-of-
magnitude constant-factor speedup for pruning at ingest time.

\subsection{Saturation is fast, and uniform}
All six fittable strata prefer exponential over power-law decay. This
was not a foregone conclusion: Heaps' law-like power-law behaviour
\citep{heaps1978} is well-documented for token vocabularies, and one
might have expected drawer-level information to inherit that structure.
It does not, at least on this corpus. Exponential saturation gives a
principled per-wing budget: solving $a e^{-\alpha N^*} = \tau$ for a
practitioner-chosen floor $\tau$ yields
$N^* = \alpha^{-1} \log(a / \tau)$. For
\texttt{\_home\_lbihari\_cognitive\_castle} with $\tau = 0.02$ that is
$N^* \approx 29$; for \texttt{projects} at the same threshold,
$N^* \approx 43$. The dominant wings hit the noise floor early. Small
wings hit it earlier still. This is the concrete argument for
smart-mining and rolling-summarization above $N^*$.

\subsection{Source-type effect is real but modest}
$\varepsilon^2 = 0.081$ says wings differ, and the fitted $\alpha$
values disagree by more than an order of magnitude across the six
strata, so it would be wrong to model the corpus as one homogeneous
distribution. But it would also be wrong to over-index on the wing
label at retrieval time: the two dominant wings sink the corpus
median, and within-wing structure (recency, session boundaries,
entity mentions) matters more than the wing label per se. Practical
takeaway: partition the pruning budget \emph{by wing}, but let
within-wing scoring do the heavy lifting.

\subsection{Protocol drives corpus more than the user does}
The two dominant wings
(\texttt{\_home\_lbihari\_cognitive\_castle} and \texttt{projects})
were populated by the Cognitive Castle mining protocol
\citep{cognitivecastle}, which walks project directories and
conversation transcripts, chunks them, and files each chunk as a
verbatim drawer. During the entire snapshot window, deliberate
user-initiated MCP filings of the form ``remember this'' totalled
roughly ten drawers, less than $0.02\%$ of the corpus. What we are
therefore characterising, at the corpus level, is the information
profile of a particular \emph{mining protocol}, not of the user's
memory-worthy utterances. Two consequences: (i) the low median A
scores in the dominant wings reflect the protocol's willingness to
file near-duplicates, not the user's redundancy; (ii) an experiment
that pitted protocol-mined drawers against user-flagged drawers
would give a much sharper H1 signal than the pooled comparison here
does.

\subsection{Future work}
The immediate next step is the H3 downstream-utility experiment on
LongMemEval \citep{longmemeval}: gate retrieval by A-score threshold
and measure $\Delta$R@5 against a no-gate baseline; the wiring is
designed but not executed. Cross-user validation is the second
priority --- $n = 1$ palace does not tell us whether $\rho(A, B) =
0.982$ replicates on a corpus with different mining protocols,
languages, or writing styles. A third direction is a same-pipeline
comparison against mem0 \citep{mem0site}, Zep \citep{zepsite}, and
Letta \citep{lettasite} storage snapshots, which would let us
report the estimator on cross-system corpora and stress-test the
exponential-decay finding.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $n = 1$ palace, one user. Absolute $\alpha$ values, medians,
    and even the sign of $\rho(A, B) - 1$ may shift on other users'
    memory patterns. Cross-user validation is the top item of
    follow-up work.
  \item Estimator dependence on \texttt{bge-m3} \citep{bgem3}: a
    different bi-encoder will produce different absolute A and B values,
    and might separate them further than the current $\rho = 0.982$.
  \item C-stage is an ordinal 1--10 rating, not a proper log-likelihood.
    The Claude CLI does not expose per-token log-probabilities, so
    \texttt{llm\_surprise} is at best a monotone proxy for entropy.
  \item Text normalization strips fenced code blocks and truncates to
    2000 tokens at ingest, so code-heavy corpora will be systematically
    under-represented.
  \item H3 downstream R@5 on LongMemEval \citep{longmemeval} is
    designed but not yet executed. The bridge from drawer-level A to
    session-level R@5 (mean over drawers per session) is one of several
    reasonable aggregations and its choice will need to be justified in
    the follow-up.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On a 65{,}779-drawer personal verbatim memory palace across seven source
wings we find (i) the cheap $O(1)$ nearest-neighbor novelty score
tracks the $O(K^3)$ LLE reconstruction residual at Spearman
$\rho = 0.982$, so production systems can prune with the cheap
estimator; (ii) exponential decay of average information beats a power
law in every fittable stratum by decisive AIC; and (iii) source-type
heterogeneity is real ($\varepsilon^2 = 0.081$) but corpus-size
saturation is the larger effect. Phase 2 will fill in the LLM-as-judge
correlations, execute the downstream R@5 experiment on
LongMemEval \citep{longmemeval}, extend the pipeline to other
personal-memory systems (mem0, Zep, Letta), and validate the per-wing
mining budget against real ingest logs. The pipeline is open-sourced
under \texttt{research/info\_theory/} in the Cognitive Castle repository
\citep{cognitivecastle}.
